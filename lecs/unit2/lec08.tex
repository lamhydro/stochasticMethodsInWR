\section{Hydrologic design}
\subsection{Generalities}
\begin{frame}{Hydrologic design}
   \begin{block}{Generalities}
   \end{block}
   \begin{itemize}
       \item \emph{Planning and management} of water resources can be classified according to their 1) \alert{use} (e.g. Hydroelectricity, water supply, recreation, etc) and their 2) \alert{control} (e.g. drainage, flood, contamination, sedimentation, etc.). 
       \item The \alert{hydrologic design} for \emph{control} is relate to extreme events of short duration (e.g. a flood). The \emph{optimal design} is a balance between the \emph{cost} and \emph{safety} of the designed structure. 
       \item Regarding that water is finite on earth,  extreme hydrological events have \emph{limit values} that are difficult to know and thus are quite uncertain. For \emph{precipitation} data, the superior limit is defined by the \emph{probable maximum precipitation (PMP)} and for \emph{streamflow} data it is defined by the \emph{probable maximum flood (PMF)}. This limits are used for instance in the \emph{design} of excess \emph{spillways} in reservoir.
       \item The estimation of the limits is deterministic so that the limits do not obey to a \emph{return period}. An structure designed based on those limits  might support all possible of hazards. 
   \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Hydrologic design}
   \begin{block}{Generalities}
   \end{block}
   \begin{itemize}
       \item When the limits are not considered, the \emph{hydrologic design} of the structure is based on \emph{frequency analysis}, where the magnitude of the hydrological variable of interest is estimated for certain \emph{design return period ($T_D$)}. For instance,   for $T_D =100$ yr the corresponding value of the design hydrological variable is expected to be exceeded in average once every 100 yr, or that the probability of the occurrence of that event in a given year is $1/T_D = 0.01$, which is quite low. 
       \item Note that when a structure is designed for a $T_D$, there is a \emph{residual hazard} because  there is a probability that a larger event occur during the \emph{structure's design life}.
       \item To estimate the magnitudes of the design hydrological variables is needed to obtain the \emph{frequency curves}; a methodology that has been explained before here. Note that the \emph{frequency curves} are obtained assuming that the data sample (e.g. annual time series of extreme rainfall) comes from a \emph{stationary process}.  
   \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Hydrologic design}
   \begin{block}{Definition of design return period ($T_D$)}
   \end{block}
   \begin{itemize}
       \item A \emph{design of a structure} can be based on the \alert{design return period ($T_D$)}. Their definition is based on \emph{design manuals} or \emph{design codes} (e.g. manual de  diseño del INVIAS o de la EAAB). Also, the $T_D$ can be established after \emph{risk analysis in hydrology} or from \emph{economical analysis}, these two allow an optimal estimate of $T_D$. 
       \item The magnitude to the hydrological design variable is obtained from the \emph{frequency curve} using $T_D$. However, it is important to follow the recommendation given by \emph{design manuals} and \emph{codes} regarding the type of \emph{probability distributions} to be implemented and the methods to estimate their parameters. For instance, in USA agencies is recommended to use the \emph{Log Pearson type III} for the analysis of \emph{maximum streamflows}. 
   \end{itemize}

   \alert{How to determine $T_D$?}\\
              \begin{itemize}
                  \item According to the \emph{type of structure}, and based on previous \emph{engineering experience} and the recommendations given by \emph{design manuals} and \emph{codes}, a value or a range of values for $T_D$ is given. For instance, for the design of \emph{highway drainage systems} it is recommended $5 yrs \leq T_D \leq 100 yrs$, where the value depends on the traffic volume. Similarly, while a $2 yrs \leq T_D \leq 25 yrs$ is recommended for \emph{pluvial sewage systems} in small cities, a $25  yrs \leq T_D \leq 50 yrs$ is recommended for large cities. 
                  \item Based on \emph{government policies}, the level of hazard can dictate the range of $T_D$. So for instance, in coastal cities, the risk of floods can be higher so for the design of  \emph{coastal levees},  $T_D$ can be up to 10000 yrs. 
              \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Hydrologic design}
   \begin{block}{Definition of design return period ($T_D$)}
   \end{block}
    \alert{How to determine $T_D$?}\\
   
    \begin{itemize}
        \item In Colombia, the \alert{RAS 2000} technical regulations, stablish that a $2  yrs \leq T_D \leq 50 yrs$ for the design of \emph{combine or pluvial sewage systems}. The ultimate value of $T_D$ will depend on the \emph{use},  \emph{slope}, area, etc of the system. The \alert{INVIAS} stablish the following $T_D$ for the design of highways structures:
                      \begin{itemize}
                          \item \emph{Gutters}: $T_D = 5 yrs$
                          \item \emph{Culvert $\phi = 0.9$ m}: $T_D = 10 yrs$
                          \item \emph{Culvert $\phi > 0.9$ m}: $T_D = 20 yrs$
                          \item \emph{Bridge $10 m \leq \text{ span } \leq 50 m$}: $T_D = 50 yrs$
                          \item \emph{Bridge $\text{ span } > 50 m$}: $T_D = 100 yrs$
    \end{itemize}
    \end{itemize}
 
    \alert{Selection of $T_D$ based on hydrological risk}\\
         To estimate $T_D$ is assumed a value for the \alert{hydrological risk ($R_h$)}. Recall that $R_h$ is defined as the probability that the hydraulic capacity of the structure is exceeded by a hydrological hazard once during the expected life of the structure ($n$ yrs). Accordingly
            \[
                T_D = \frac{1}{1- (1-R_h)^{1/n}}
            \]

\end{frame}

   \vspace{-5pt}
    \alert{Selection of $T_D$ based on economical analysis}\\
    The effects of the occurrence of \emph{extreme hydrological events} on  the population and  economy change depending on the event and population characteristics. To establish an optimal $T_D$ is necessary to perform an \emph{economical analysis}. There are two types: \alert{ the analysis of minimal cost} and \alert{ the analysis of cost-benefits}.
    \begin{itemize} 
        \item \emph{\underline{Analysis of minimal cost}}: The higher $T_D$ the higher the cost of the control measures (\emph{capital costs ($C_C$)}) adopted to reduce the risk (e.g. the construction of a most robust structure). Accordingly

    \item The figure (a), $C_C$ vs $T_D$,  shows that the annual capital cost $Cc$ increase when $T_D$ increase.

   The  \emph{curve of exceedance probability vs damages} shows the resulting damage if a flood with a $T_R$ occurs. If the \emph{design streamflow ($Q_D$)} is associated to a $T_D$, the structure would be able to prevent those damages caused by $Q\leq Q_D$, but not for $Q > Q_D$. This means that the \emph{expected annual damage $D_E$} (due to the residual risk) in a given $T_D$ is
\[
    D_E (T_D) = \int_{Q_D}^\infty D(q) f_Q (q) dq
\]

 \begin{minipage}{0.58\textwidth}
where $D(q)$ is the associated damage to the occurrence of $Q=q$ and $f_Q (q)$ is the \emph{pdf} of the maximum streamflows. Note that $1/T_R$ is equivalent to $1-F_Q(q)$, where $F_Q(q)$ is the \emph{cdf} of $Q$. It is important to mention that $f_Q(q)$ is well known, so $Q_{T_R}$ is certain. This equation represents the area under the curve between 0 and $1/T_D = 1/T_R$. The curve of $D_E$ vs $T_D$ is built using the equation above for different values of $T_D$. Note that the curves above have uncertainties, so the uncertainties associated to the different curves used to build $D_E$ vs $T_D$ are shown in the figure.

\begin{frame}{Hydrologic design}
   \begin{block}{Definition of design return period ($T_D$)}
   \end{block}
   \begin{itemize}
       \item \emph{\underline{Analysis of cost-benefits}}: this analysis requires to consider all costs and benefits of the risk management policies in order to:
           \begin{enumerate}
               \item stablish the risk scenario (how large are the damages per year)
                \item determine the potential risk mitigation measures and their costs
                \item compare the benefits and the costs of reducing risk (are the benefits larger than the costs?)
                \item compare the relationships between costs and benefits for the different measures (which bring larger net benefits?)
           \end{enumerate}
           The \emph{Analysis of cost-benefits} for flood events is made based on the flood type and its characteristics (e.g. water depth, duration, flow speed, rate of water rise, frequency, contamination, etc.). The analysis try to quantify the variety of damages and their associated costs, during and after the occurrence of the extreme event. According to the USA Army Corsps of Engineers, the benefits are classified as:
           \begin{enumerate}
               \item Benefits due to flood mitigation ($B_{IR}$) that reduce economic losses. 
               \item Benefits due to increments  of activities ($B_I$) due to  implemented measures.
               \item Benefits due to increments of incomes ($B_L$) due to the implemented measures (e.g. can bring more development to the area).
           \end{enumerate}
   \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Hydrologic design}
   \begin{block}{Definition of design return period ($T_D$)}
   \end{block}
   \begin{itemize}
\item \emph{\underline{Analysis of cost-benefits}}:
          Accordingly, the economic efficiency due to the implementation of one or multiple mitigation measures for flood events can be estimated as a function of $T_D$ as
          \[
              BN (T_D) = B_{IR} (T_D) + B_{I} (T_D) + B_{L} (T_D) - C(T_D)
          \]
          where $BN$ is the \emph{net benefit} and $C$ represents the \emph{total cost} to implement, operate, and maintain the adopted measure(s). $B_{IR} (T_D) = D_E (T_D)|_{without} - D_E (T_D)|_{with} $, this is the difference between the damages without the control measures (current conditions) and the damages with the control measures (estimated using the equation for $D_E (T_D)$).
   \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Hydrologic design}
    \begin{block}{Measures to mitigate flood damage}
   \end{block}
   \emph{Floods} are perhaps the most common and frequent extreme hydrological events. Some measures to mitigate their effects are presented here that might help in the context of hydrologic design. These measures are classified as:
   \begin{enumerate}
       \item \alert{structural}: these seeks to reduce damages by \emph{controlling the water flowing} into the floodplain using reservoirs to store and delay the inflowing water volumes, \emph{increasing the capacity of channels} to transport flow volumes, and \emph{building dikes and diversions}. 
       \item \alert{non structural}: Community preventing actions oriented towards \emph{reducing the susceptibility to damages} due to floods, implementation of \emph{early warning systems} to enhance preparedness and \emph{regulation policies} related to land usages and relocation. 
   \end{enumerate}
\end{frame}
